*Committee chairs, not party leaders, are where a bill actually lives or dies — gaveling hearings, writing the text, deciding whether something gets a floor vote at all. This audit holds the chairs of the House and Senate's five most consequential committees — Appropriations, Judiciary, Rules, Ways and Means/Finance, and Armed Services — to the same yardstick as the leadership audit: floor attendance, bills personally sponsored that became law, the industries funding their campaigns, and anything on the public record. Reporting on documents, not legal advice.*

## The pattern

The nine chairs split into two groups that have nothing to do with party. Five of them — Cole, Smith, Collins, Grassley, Wicker — post strong attendance and real legislative output, with donor profiles that track their committee's jurisdiction rather than any one dominant relationship. The other four carry a specific, documented flag: Jordan has chaired Judiciary since 2023 and has never had a single personally sponsored bill signed into law in eighteen years in Congress; Foxx's top donor industry is the for-profit college sector she is supposed to police; Rogers is the single largest national recipient of defense-industry money while chairing the committee that writes the defense budget; Crapo's top donor industry is securities and investment while he chairs the committee that writes tax and trade law for that same industry.

None of that is illegal. Committee assignments and donor interest tracking the same industry is how the system is built, not evidence a chair broke a rule. But a Judiciary chairman with a legislative record of zero enacted bills, and an Armed Services chairman who is defense's single largest House recipient, are both facts worth printing plainly rather than folding into an average.

## The scored card

The same warning as always: **attendance percentages, bill counts, and donor dollar figures are measurements**, sourced to GovTrack, Congress.gov, or OpenSecrets/FEC filings. **The final grade is not.** It is the journal's editorial blend of those measurements into a 0–100 judgment of the whole record. We publish the inputs so you can argue with the math.

Scale: **85+** strong record, no red flags · **65–84** solid record, real but limited questions · **45–64** mixed — real conflicts alongside real output · **25–44** weak record, major concerns · **under 25** actively derelict or compromised.

Nearly identical to the leadership average, and for similar reasons. Five of the nine chairs post attendance and output numbers that would rank near the top of any Congress; the other four carry a specific, sourced flag apiece rather than a pattern of universal rot. This is not a committee system in crisis. It is a committee system doing exactly what its structure predicts — assigning power over an industry to the people that industry already funds — with the House Judiciary chairman's flat legislative record standing out as the one number on this card that has nothing to do with donors at all.

## The last word

Two findings carry more weight than the average. First: on four of the nine cards, donor concentration lines up precisely with committee jurisdiction — Foxx and for-profit colleges, Rogers and defense contractors, Crapo and securities firms, echoing Barrasso-and-energy and Emmer-and-crypto from the leadership audit. That correlation is the committee system functioning as designed, for better and worse, and we don't print it as a crime because it isn't one. Second: Jim Jordan's zero-bills-enacted record, spanning eighteen years and both his time in the minority and his current chairmanship, is the single starkest legislative-output number in either audit published so far — worth sitting with regardless of what one thinks of the committee's other work.

*Ubi non est lex, non est transgressio* — where there is no law, there is no transgression. Nothing on this card rises to a crime. What it rises to is a record, printed plainly, of who funds the people who chair the committees that write the laws.
