*Congress's power to investigate the executive branch is one of the oldest tools in its kit — and one of the weakest in practice. This audit grades the actual machinery of congressional oversight: the subpoena power itself, the two paths to enforce it, and the privilege doctrine that increasingly blocks it. Same method as every institutional entry in this series — one design feature at a time, scored 0–100, every measurement cited. Reporting on documents, not legal advice.*

## The pattern

Congress's power to demand documents and testimony is on solid constitutional ground — courts settled that a century ago. What's broken is everything downstream of the subpoena itself. Congress has exactly two ways to make a defiant witness comply: ask the Justice Department to prosecute, or sue in civil court. The first runs through the same executive branch Congress is often investigating, and it shows: DOJ has sought indictment in only 2 of the last 10 criminal contempt referrals since 2008. The second can take years to resolve. And Congress's own power to arrest and detain a witness directly — no DOJ, no courts, just the Sergeant at Arms — has not been used since 1935. The subpoena arrives with real legal force. What happens if you ignore it is almost entirely a matter of who's in the White House when you do.

<p class="scorecard__judgment">By statute, a U.S. Attorney has "the duty" to bring a House contempt citation before a grand jury. In practice, DOJ has never treated that as mandatory — it claims full prosecutorial discretion over every referral. The record shows what that discretion means: since 2008, the House has voted ten people in criminal contempt of Congress, and DOJ sought indictment in exactly two of them. Both Democratic and Republican administrations have consistently declined to prosecute executive branch officials who withheld material on a president's order, regardless of which party controlled the White House at the time.</p>

<div class="scorecard__head"><span class="scorecard__num">3</span><h3 class="scorecard__title">Inherent contempt</h3><span class="scorecard__band">Congress's own arrest power</span></div>
<div class="scorecard__meter scorecard__meter--low"><span style="width:12%"></span></div>
<p class="scorecard__judgment">The lowest score on this card. Congress has an independent power to detain and imprison a witness itself, through its own Sergeant at Arms, with no need for DOJ or the courts at all. It has not been used since 1935. In a series of 2024 opinions, the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel went further, concluding Congress cannot constitutionally use inherent contempt to arrest, fine, or otherwise punish an executive branch official who is withholding material under a presidential claim of executive privilege — an opinion Congress never agreed to and has no mechanism to overrule short of a court fight it has never brought. A tool nine decades dormant, that the executive branch's own lawyers have now told Congress not to pick back up.</p>

The House Judiciary Committee's suit to compel former White House Counsel Don McGahn's testimony took roughly two years to work through the courts before McGahn ultimately testified, by which point the underlying investigation had lost most of its urgency. A legal path that works, on a timeline that frequently defeats the purpose of oversight in the first place.</p>

<p class="scorecard__judgment">Executive privilege is a real, court-recognized doctrine, not an invented excuse — United States v. The problem for oversight is how it's used in practice: privilege claims are the leading reason DOJ cites for declining contempt prosecutions of executive officials, and the assertion itself, valid or not, routinely stalls a subpoena for the length of a court fight. A legitimate doctrine that has become the default first move against nearly any subpoena a White House doesn't like.</p>

<div class="scorecard__meter scorecard__meter--low"><span style="width:22%"></span></div>
<p class="scorecard__judgment">Steve Bannon was convicted of criminal contempt of Congress in July 2022 for defying a January 6 committee subpoena, and a D.C. Circuit panel upheld that conviction in May 2024. After the change in administration, DOJ reversed its own position — in a February 2026 brief, the same Justice Department that won the conviction argued the original subpoena had been unlawful. On April 6, 2026, the Supreme Court set aside the appellate ruling and sent the case back down, clearing the way for DOJ to move to dismiss the case it had previously prosecuted to a conviction and a served prison sentence. Whether contempt of Congress gets enforced now depends less on whether a witness defied a lawful subpoena than on which administration is deciding whether to defend the conviction.</p>

The lowest grade in the Capitol Audit so far, and the shape of the card explains why. The subpoena power that starts the process is on rock-solid legal ground, scoring higher than almost anything else audited in this series. Everything that happens after a witness says no collapses fast: a criminal referral process where the prosecuting decision belongs to the very branch under investigation, an arrest power Congress has left in the drawer for ninety years, a civil court path that outlasts most news cycles, and a privilege doctrine that increasingly does the executive branch's blocking for it. Bannon's case is the cleanest illustration of the whole pattern — the same conduct, the same statute, two different Justice Departments reaching opposite conclusions about whether it was a crime at all.

## The last word

Two findings matter more than the average. First: Congress has genuine, court-affirmed authority to demand answers from the executive branch, but almost none of the authority to make anyone actually give them — every enforcement path runs either through the branch being investigated or through a court system too slow to matter in real time. Second: the Bannon reversal shows the system isn't just weak, it's inconsistent in a specific, predictable direction — contempt gets enforced against a defiant witness only when the administration bringing the case isn't the one whose official defied the subpoena.

*Ubi non est lex, non est transgressio* — where there is no law, there is no transgression. The law here is real. What's missing is anyone with a consistent interest in enforcing it.
